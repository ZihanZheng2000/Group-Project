# Needfinding Plan for Settling Shared Expenses

*Team project working document  English version  November 12 2026*

## 1 What are we studying

Our focal design situation is a short activity shared by three to five friends, with at least two expenses, one or more people paying upfront, and settlement afterward in one currency. For example, four friends take a short trip and later work out what each person owes, whom to pay, and whether both the money and any shared record are settled. They may use Splitwise, a group chat, a spreadsheet, or another method. The survey casts a wider net: its first question requires an activity with friends in the past year, at least three people sharing at least two expenses, and someone paying upfront. It does not screen for an upper group-size limit, trip length, one currency, or an existing shared expense list.

The settlement task starts when a group begins working out its shared costs, whether it already has a list or must gather expenses at that point. It ends when the people involved can tell whether money has been sent and received, whether any shared record matches what happened, and whether a balance remains unresolved. We use five stages to look for problems: (1) check whether all expenses are included; (2) understand what each person owes or is owed; (3) check the amount and raise questions before paying; (4) pay and communicate or record what happened; and (5) decide whether everything is settled or deal with an exception. These stages guide the research; they do not assume which stage is hardest. Stage 1 covers how missing or changed expenses affect settlement, while stage 2 covers whether people understand the amount. Stages 3 to 5 remain the main interaction of interest. If the evidence points elsewhere, we will adjust the focus.

We will study the settlement task from beginning to end. Survey answers may draw on several qualifying experiences, while the Splitwise review follows one fixed scenario. We will not take on ongoing housemate accounts, currency conversion, receipt scanning, or the details of making a bank transfer unless they directly affect settlement. For the prototype, we expect to support one ordinary path and one important exception, chosen from the evidence.

Our main questions are: What do people actually do at each stage? Where do they face extra work, uncertainty, mistakes, or awkward conversations? How do people distinguish a payment being sent, received, and reflected in a shared record? How do they notice and fix problems now, and what does that take? The survey does not collect payment roles, so it cannot establish differences between payers and recipients. Our first observations of Splitwise give us ideas to check; they are not, by themselves, evidence of user needs.

## 2 Activity one  Survey  Wu

**Recruitment and target size.** We aim for about 20 valid responses from classmates and personal contacts with at least one qualifying experience in the past year. We will seek a mix of people who used an expense-sharing app and people who used other methods. We may invite people with different payment roles, but the final survey does not ask their role, so we cannot describe or compare role groups from its answers. Question 1 ends the survey for No or Not sure. We will report the numbers received, screened out, and retained. The survey's broader screen does not establish that respondents fit the narrower three-to-five-person, short-activity, one-currency design situation. A convenience sample of around 20 people cannot represent all users.

**What participants will do.** Participants may draw on one or more qualifying experiences from the past year; different questions may refer to different experiences. They select everything they remember happening and choose one clear example for question 14. The final survey has 13 choice questions (questions 1–13) and two optional written questions (14–15). Question 1 screens eligibility, and question 2 asks what tools or methods groups used. The five stages then cover missing or changed expenses (question 3); how amounts were worked out and what was hard to understand (4–5); checks and questions before payment (6–7); payment or offsets and how these were communicated or recorded (8–9); and evidence of completion or mismatches (10–11). Question 12 asks what actually happened during settlement, and question 13 asks which steps took effort or left uncertainty; neither asks for a single hardest step. Question 14 asks for one difficult moment, actions, outcome, and possible help; question 15 asks what difficulties or needs the choices missed. Before launch, two or three people will pilot the survey to check wording, select-alone options, and completion time. We will use the pilot to set a realistic time estimate and refine wording without changing the meaning of the questions.

**Recording and analysis.** We will not ask for names, account details, or exact amounts. For each choice question, we will report the number who answered it and the count and percentage choosing each option. For select-all-that-apply questions, the denominator is the number answering that question, so percentages need not total 100%. A selected option means a respondent recalls it happening in at least one qualifying experience; it is not an event-level rate. We may describe answers by tool or method selected in question 2, noting that respondents can select several methods and groups may overlap. The survey contains no role question, so it cannot support role comparisons. We will code optional written answers by stage, difficulty, action or workaround, and effect, retaining experiences outside the five stages. Questions 12 and 13 describe recalled effects and effort or uncertainty, while question 14 may explain one moment in context. We cannot infer that choices on different questions happened together, in a particular order, or in the same settlement, and we cannot estimate prevalence among all users.

**Possible biases.** Classmates and personal contacts form a convenience sample. Recalling one or more experiences across a year may favor memorable problems and blur which event a detail belongs to. We hear only one person's account and cannot verify whether another friend received the money. The No problem and Cannot remember options help people avoid guessing, and the optional example adds context, but neither removes these limits. The wider screen also means some answers may come from settings outside our narrower design situation.

## 3 Activity two  Usability review of Splitwise  Jiayi

**Fixed scenario and materials.** Jiayi will carry out a heuristic evaluation of Splitwise using fictional expenses from a short trip taken by four friends. Different people pay for the meal, transport, and tickets; one expense is shared by only some members; and all amounts use one currency. Before starting, the reviewer will record the device, app version, region, and account conditions. Our earlier trial notes provide points to check, but the formal review will record what actually appears while working through the fixed task. No real money will be transferred.

**Task sequence.** Starting with existing expenses and an amount to settle, the reviewer will try to notice an expense that was added or changed; trace where a person's balance and payee came from; check an expense shared by only some members; find how to settle up; simulate recording a payment made outside the app; check the resulting status; and correct a payment recorded by mistake. At each point, the reviewer will record the action, what the interface showed, screenshots, obstacles, and possible consequences. The review will use at least six usability principles: whether the system's status is visible; whether its language matches real-world debt, transfer, and receipt of money; whether it prevents errors; whether people can see the information they need without relying on memory; whether they can undo or change an action; and whether errors are easy to understand and recover from.

**Possible biases.** Knowing the problems seen in our first trial may lead the reviewer to look mainly for those problems. One region or app version also cannot stand for every user's experience. The notes will separate what the interface actually showed from what we think a user might misunderstand. The latter must be checked against the survey and reviews.

## 4 Activity three  Analysis of public reviews  Zilin

**Materials and screening.** Zilin will use the 50 public Splitwise reviews the team has already collected. They are a convenience sample from different platforms and years, and the current document contains Chinese summaries. Each review will first be checked for whether it describes settling expenses after a short group activity. A review from another setting, such as an ongoing housemate account, may still help explain the same underlying problem; it will be marked as an adjacent setting. Reviews with too little context will be marked uncertain. Comments about pricing, subscriptions, or receipt scanning will enter the main analysis only when the reviewer clearly says they got in the way of this settlement task. Before quoting a specific case, Zilin will check the original review for meaning, date, and any clue about the app version.

**Coding.** For each relevant review, Zilin will record the stage or stages involved, what the reviewer did, the obstacle, its effect, and any workaround or fix. One review can cover more than one stage. We will not guess the group size, payment method, or cause when the summary does not say. We will describe the different kinds of problems found at each stage and keep positive or trouble-free accounts that challenge our expectations. A feature suggested by a reviewer is that person's proposal, not automatically a feature the team should build.

**Possible biases.** People who post reviews may be especially happy or unhappy. Older reviews may describe an interface, price, or feature that has changed. Counts within these 50 reviews describe only this collection; they cannot tell us how often a problem occurs among all users.

## 5 Bringing the three sources together

We will organize findings in a table with the five task stages as rows and the three evidence sources as columns. For each possible finding, we will say what respondents recalled in the survey, what the interface did in the heuristic review, and whether public reviews contain similar or conflicting experiences. For example, a respondent might recall a shared record showing a debt after a transfer; the interface review might show that an outside payment must be entered manually; and a public review might mention forgetting that step. Together, these are a reason to investigate the gap between payment and record updates. They do not describe the same event and cannot be added into a prevalence estimate. When sources disagree, we will keep the disagreement and look for differences in context.

The team will then decide which stage has the clearest effect on settlement in the focal situation, who is affected, what happens, and how hard the current workaround is. We will identify at least three task insights supported by evidence that we could not know for certain beforehand, then choose a focus for the prototype. The three methods partly offset each other's weaknesses, but convenience sampling, memory gaps, self-selected reviews, and a heuristic review limited to Splitwise will remain. In Needfinding Results, we will explain any difference between this plan and what we actually did, and we will not present a hypothesis as a finding.
