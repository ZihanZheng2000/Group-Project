\section{Initial Brainstorming Plan}

All five members will review the findings from our survey, heuristic evaluation, and product review analysis, then revisit our individual ideas and shared Splitwise trial. We will focus on the shared-expense settlement process, from reviewing balances and correcting expenses to following up on payments. Each member willgenerate ideas independently before group discussion.

We will follow four rules:
(1) \textit{Expressiveness}: welcome unusual or unfinished ideas;
(2) \textit{Nonevaluation}: avoid criticism during idea generation;
(3) \textit{Quantity}: generate as many ideas as possible; and
(4) \textit{Building}: expand on others' suggestions.

Starting independently should reduce early conformity. Taking turns gives everyone space to speak, while postponing criticism helps reduce evaluation apprehension. To address confirmation bias, we will compare ideas against findings from all three methods, including successful settlement experiences. We will also ask whether new controls or confirmation steps add unnecessary work.

After generating ideas, we will evaluate ideas based on user needs, task clarity, user effort, and prototyping feasibility, then select three alternatives for medium-fidelity prototypes. Each prototype will cover the organizer's full workflow, with unchanged steps matching the baseline for later comparison.

\section{Brainstorming Results and Design Alternatives}

During brainstorming, we identified three key challenges in settling up: understanding how balances are calculated, correcting expenses after payments begin, and following up on unpaid balances. We discussed five ways to address these situations and chose three for medium-fidelity prototypes.

\textbf{Prototype 1: A personal settlement breakdown.} A person in the group sees whom they need to pay and how much. If the amount seems higher than expected, they can open it to see which expenses, such as dinner and transportation, make up the total. Opening the dinner expense shows the items imported from its scanned receipt, the tax, the tip, and who shares each item. This lets the person check the amount and point to the exact expense or item if something is wrong. We will test whether the breakdown answers people\textquotesingle s questions while still letting those with no questions settle quickly.

\textbf{Prototype 2: Change requests handled by the expense creator.} Suppose a dinner expense for a group of four was split four ways, but only three friends attended. A group member can request a change on that expense and explain who should be included. The person who created the expense sees each person\textquotesingle s amount before and after the proposed change, then approves or rejects the request.. The affected members see the decision and their updated balances, including anyone who already paid the old amount. We will test whether the request and its result are easy to understand, and whether waiting for the creator delays settlement.

\textbf{Prototype 3: Payment reminders and status updates.} A recipient sees that a friend still has an outstanding payment and sends a reminder that shows the amount and related expenses. The friend can reply that they will pay later or that they have already sent a bank transfer. In the second case, the recipient checks whether the money arrived and updates the shared record. If the money has not arrived or the amount is in question, they can keep working through that payment. We will test whether reminders help people finish settling up and notice differences between payments and records, and whether the reminder feels too pushy.

All three prototypes use the same group of friends and the same recorded expenses. The receipts have already been scanned before the scenario begins, so the prototypes focus on settling up. Each prototype then uses the situation that best tests its idea: checking an amount, correcting a split, or responding to a reminder. This gives participants a concrete task and lets us compare the help each design provides with the extra work it adds.


\section{Initial Prototyping}
Based on our needfinding results, we proposed three design alternatives to improve the overall user experience of Splitwise when reviewing, settling, and managing shared expenses. Each alternative addresses a different usability issue identified in the research.

\subsection{Design Alternative 1: Detailed Expense and Balance Breakdown}
Our needfinding results showed that users had difficulty understanding how their balances were calculated. The first prototype aims to address the problem of insufficient transparency in expense and balance information. On the individual expense page, users can see the total amount, the payer, and the amount each person needs to pay. Users can also expand a section to view more details, such as the subtotal, taxes, tips, and how the total is divided among group members.

If the total balance between two users is made up of multiple expenses, users can click "View Breakdown" on the balance page to view all the expenses that make up the total balance. Each expense can also be expanded to view the details without leaving the current page.

To avoid showing too much information at once, the main page only displays a summary, and detailed information is shown only when users need it. This follows the HCI principles of recognition rather than recall and simplicity. It helps users understand the total balance without requiring them to search through multiple expenses.

\subsection{Design Alternative 2: Controlled Editing and Change Requests}
Our heuristic evaluation found that users could modify or delete payment records involving other group members. The second prototype aims to prevent expenses and payment records from being unexpectedly modified. For expenses, only the creator can directly modify them. If other members find any errors, they can click "Request Change" to submit a change request, and the creator can decide whether to approve or reject it.

For payment records, modifications or deletions require confirmation from both the payer and the recipient. Other group members cannot directly modify or delete them, but if any errors are found, they can submit a change request. Once a change is approved, the affected members can see their updated balances.

The prototype shows the process of submitting a request, reviewing the proposed change, and displaying the approval status. This design follows the HCI principles of error prevention and user control and freedom by reducing unexpected changes while still allowing other users to correct mistakes.

\subsection{Design Alternative 3: Customizable Payment Reminders}
Our survey results showed that some users struggled with reminding others about unpaid expenses and  waiting for a response. The third prototype focuses on reminders settings rather than implementing the full two-way conversation proposed during brainstorming. Expense creators can set reminders for unpaid balances, choosing from preset options of 1 day, 3 days, 5 days, or 30 days, or setting a custom interval.  Users can view, modify, or cancel reminders, which automatically stop when a payment is marked as completed.


This design reduces the need for users to manually send reminders and makes it easier for everyone to track unpaid balances. The design supports visibility of system status and user control by making reminder settings and payment status clear.
